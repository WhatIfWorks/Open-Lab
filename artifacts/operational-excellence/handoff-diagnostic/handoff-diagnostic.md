## Using the Condensed Worksheet with This Companion

The DOCX/PDF worksheet condenses the nine diagnostic lenses into a practical capture format. This companion retains expanded investigative questions to help explore a handoff in greater depth. Use the questions that improve understanding rather than treating every prompt as a mandatory checklist.

### Handoff Map

In worksheet section B, identify the sending and receiving team, function, or person. Describe what crosses the boundary, the mechanism/channel, the trigger that starts the handoff, and the expected next activity. The map makes the interface visible; it is not a substitute for a complete process map.

### Independent Descriptions and Comparison

In worksheet section C, capture brief factual statements under “Sending side says...” and “Receiving side says...” before reconciling the accounts. Compare where the views agree, where they differ, and what assumptions become visible. Preserve differences as evidence to investigate rather than forcing immediate agreement.

### Synthesis Outputs

The worksheet's Synthesize area captures:

1. Where the views agree.
2. Where they differ.
3. What assumptions became visible.
4. What deserves deeper investigation.
5. What evidence would test that.
6. What should not be assumed yet.

The expected outputs are a handoff map, two independent descriptions, observations/evidence across the nine lenses, and a synthesis identifying further investigation and evidence needs. They are not a handoff score or proof of root cause.

### Limitations

This is not a validated assessment, regulatory standard, maturity model, substitute for process mapping, root cause analysis method, or scoring system. The diagnostic helps make interfaces visible; it does not prove that a handoff caused a problem.

**Historical artifact identifier:** Open Lab #001

A handoff occurs whenever work, information, responsibility, authority, material, or a decision crosses a boundary.

That boundary might exist between:

- two people;
- two teams;
- two departments;
- two systems;
- two locations;
- two shifts;
- two organizations;
- or two stages of the same process.

When something goes wrong, we naturally look at the processes on either side.

Sometimes the more useful place to look is the space between them.

## 1. Transfer

What exactly crosses the boundary?

Ask:

- Is it clear what is being transferred?
- Is the expected content or condition defined?
- Does the receiving side know what "ready" looks like?
- Does the sending side use the same definition?
- Is anything routinely missing, incomplete, or ambiguous?
- Can the receiver determine whether the handoff is complete?

## 2. Mechanism

A well-defined handoff can still fail if the way it travels is unreliable.

Ask:

- How does the handoff actually occur?
- Is the mechanism formal, informal, or both?
- Does more than one channel carry parts of the handoff?
- Can one part arrive without another?
- Is the handoff visible and traceable?
- Does the mechanism confirm delivery or only transmission?
- Are people compensating for weaknesses in the mechanism through memory, relationships, or workarounds?

## 3. Ownership

A handoff can create gaps, overlaps, or ambiguity in ownership.

Ask:

- Who owns the work immediately before the handoff?
- Who owns it immediately after?
- Is there a point where nobody clearly owns it?
- Is there a point where both sides believe they own it?
- Does responsibility transfer at the same moment as authority?
- Does the sender believe responsibility ends before the receiver believes responsibility begins?
- Who is responsible for confirming receipt or acceptance?
- Who has authority to act when the handoff fails?

## 4. Information

The work may transfer successfully while the information needed to act on it does not.

Ask:

- What information must travel with the handoff?
- Is that information available when needed?
- Is important context being communicated informally?
- Does the receiver know where the information came from?
- Can the receiver distinguish complete information from incomplete information?
- Are people compensating for missing information through experience or tribal knowledge?

## 5. Expectations

Both sides may be performing exactly as they believe they should while operating from different expectations.

Ask:

- What does the sender expect the receiver to do?
- What does the receiver believe the sender should have done first?
- Are timing expectations shared?
- Are quality or completeness expectations shared?
- Are priorities interpreted the same way?
- What does each side consider a successful handoff?

## 6. Timing

Many handoff failures are not failures of execution.

They are failures of synchronization.

Ask:

- When is the receiver expecting the handoff?
- When does the sender believe it is due?
- What triggers the handoff?
- Are there dependencies that either side cannot see?
- What happens when the work arrives early?
- What happens when it arrives late?
- Is urgency communicated consistently?

## 7. Acceptance

Sending something does not necessarily mean it has been successfully handed off.

Ask:

- How does the receiver know the handoff has occurred?
- How does the sender know it was received?
- What determines whether the handoff is accepted?
- Can the receiver reject or return it?
- Are acceptance criteria explicit or assumed?
- Does acceptance formally transfer ownership?
- Is the receiver actually ready to act?
- Are required access, resources, prerequisites, or capacity available?
- Can something be technically accepted while still not being operationally ready?

## 8. Exceptions

The normal path may work perfectly.

The exception path may not exist.

Ask:

- What happens when the handoff is incomplete?
- What happens when the receiver cannot accept it?
- Can the work be returned?
- Who resolves disagreements?
- Is there a defined escalation path?
- Do exceptions create workarounds outside the normal process?
- Are recurring exceptions being treated as individual events rather than system signals?

>

## 9. Feedback

A system cannot improve if information only travels in one direction.

Ask:

- Does the sender know whether the handoff worked?
- Can the receiver easily provide feedback?
- Does feedback reach someone capable of changing the upstream process?
- Are recurring problems visible across the boundary?
- Is feedback treated as useful information or as criticism?
- Does anyone look for patterns across multiple handoff failures?

## What Is the Handoff Telling You?

Do not jump immediately to corrective action.

First ask:

**Is the problem actually inside one of the processes, or at the interface between them?**

Then consider:

- Is the transfer unclear?
- Is the mechanism unreliable or invisible?
- Is ownership or authority unclear?
- Is necessary information incomplete or disconnected from the work?
- Are expectations different across the boundary?
- Is timing poorly synchronized?
- Is acceptance assumed rather than explicit?
- Is the receiver actually ready to act?
- Is the exception path weak or undefined?
- Is feedback unable to travel upstream?
- Is the formal process different from how the work actually happens?

There may be more than one answer.

There may also be something important that this diagnostic never asked.

Add it.

## Don't Score It Yet

This version intentionally does not include a score.

A numerical score can create the appearance of precision before we know whether these questions represent meaningful dimensions of handoff performance.

Use the diagnostic first.

See what it reveals.

If scoring would genuinely improve the tool, that can be tested later.
